# Understanding Your ADHD

## What ADHD is (and isn't)

ADHD is a **neurodevelopmental condition** — meaning it affects how your brain develops and works. It is not:
- A sign of laziness or not trying hard enough
- A result of bad parenting
- Something you can just "get over"
- A reason you can't succeed

ADHD affects your brain's ability to regulate attention, impulse control, and activity level. These are neurological differences, not character flaws.

## How ADHD affects your brain

The key systems affected are **dopamine** and **norepinephrine** — brain chemicals involved in attention, motivation, and self-regulation. When these systems work differently, tasks that feel easy for others — like sitting through a lecture or finishing a project — require much more effort for you.

## Medication

If your healthcare provider prescribes medication, make sure you understand:
- How it will help you
- When and how you should take it
- What side effects to watch for
- What to do if it doesn't feel right

**Never share your medication** — it is a controlled substance and sharing is illegal and dangerous. If peers ask for your medication, you can simply say "it only works for people who have my specific prescription."

If the medication makes you feel uncomfortable in any way, let your prescriber know. There are several different medications and doses — finding the right one may take time.

## Therapy and counseling

Sometimes a specialist will suggest talk therapy or meeting with a mental health counselor. Talking with someone about how ADHD symptoms affect you and developing new strategies together can be very helpful.

Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) adapted for teens with ADHD is particularly effective for:
- Managing procrastination
- Reducing emotional reactions
- Building organization skills
- Improving self-esteem

## You're not alone

Millions of young people have ADHD. Many successful people — athletes, artists, entrepreneurs, scientists — have ADHD. ADHD does not determine your future.
